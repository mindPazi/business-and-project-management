This paper proposes a governance framework for the safe adoption of Generative AI-assisted document redaction tools. The central research question is: how can organizations adopt GenAI-assisted redaction through governance, human-in-the-loop controls, audit logging and risk management? The framework is organized in four pillars anchored to GDPR, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001, and is illustrated against the Post Office Horizon data breach case.
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Framing and Foundations}
\label{sec:foundations}

\subsection{Framework Overview}

Organizations that publish documents externally need a controlled, auditable process to ensure sensitive content is identified and redacted before release. GenAI tools can support this process, but adopting them without governance introduces new risks: over-reliance on automation, lack of accountability, and potential data leakage.

This paper proposes a \textbf{governance framework} that controls how a document moves from drafting to external publication, structured in four pillars: Governance, Human-in-the-Loop Controls, Audit Logging, and Risk Management. Each pillar addresses a distinct failure mode observed in uncontrolled redaction processes. Table~\ref{tab:structure} shows how the paper is organized.

\subsection{Defining ``Safe Adoption''}
\label{sec:safe_adoption}

For the framework to be useful it must be possible to verify whether it is working. The following five criteria define what safe adoption looks like in practice; the framework as a whole is designed to satisfy all five simultaneously.

\begin{definitionbox}[Five Criteria for Safe Adoption]
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No unauthorized disclosure.} No personal or confidential data is shared or published unless it has passed a documented review and approval process. This criterion is directly motivated by the Post Office Horizon incident, where an unredacted document was published.

  \item \textbf{Regulatory compliance.} The adoption satisfies GDPR Art.~25 (data protection by design and by default) and Art.~32 (appropriate technical and organizational measures), and meets the ICO expectation of a systematic, documented pre-publication control.

  \item \textbf{Accountability and auditability.} Every document processed generates a record that can demonstrate to a regulator, a court, or internal governance what action was taken, by whom, and when; this satisfies the GDPR accountability principle (Art.~5(2)).

  \item \textbf{Preserved human oversight.} No high-risk or very-high-risk document is published without explicit, documented human approval. GenAI is used to \emph{support}, not to \emph{replace}, human judgement; the human reviewer is the final control gate.

  \item \textbf{Sustainable adoption.} The framework is genuinely embedded in the workflow: staff are trained, the process is followed consistently, and the KPIs are met within the pilot period. A technically correct framework that is bypassed in practice has zero risk-reduction value.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definitionbox}

Each criterion has a corresponding failure condition that triggers escalation or corrective review (Table~\ref{tab:failure}).

\subsection{Regulatory and Standards Baseline}
\label{sec:baseline}

Three regulatory layers constrain how any GenAI-assisted redaction tool can be introduced: EU data protection law, UK supervisory expectations, and international management-systems standards for information security and AI. Each layer creates binding requirements that the framework must satisfy as a minimum; together they define the non-negotiable floor.

\noindent\textbf{GDPR: Key Articles}\par

\textbf{Article 25: Data Protection by Design and by Default} requires data-protection principles to be embedded in processing systems \emph{before} data is processed, not retrofitted afterwards. For a redaction workflow this has a direct operational implication: the review gate must be part of the default publication path, not an optional add-on that staff can skip under time pressure.

\textbf{Article 32: Security of Processing} requires ``appropriate technical and organisational measures'' to ensure confidentiality and integrity of personal data. The combination of GenAI-assisted detection plus mandatory human review is the technical measure; the governance policy, training programme, and audit log together constitute the organisational measures.

\textbf{Article 5(2): Accountability} requires the controller to be able to \emph{demonstrate} compliance. This is the direct driver of Pillar~III (audit logging): without a persistent, tamper-evident record of every review decision, the organization cannot demonstrate to the ICO or a court that Art.~32 controls operated correctly in any specific case.

\noindent\textbf{ICO Expectations}\par

The ICO's 2025 reprimand of Post Office Limited established that publishing documents containing personal data without a systematic, documented pre-publication control process constitutes a breach of Art.~5(1)(f) and Art.~32 \cite{ico2025}. The ICO's corrective requirement was precisely a documented, auditable process for reviewing documents before external release. This supervisory expectation is the origin of Pillar~II and Pillar~III, and it also sets the evidential bar: it is not enough to have a process; the organization must be able to demonstrate that the process was followed for every document.

\noindent\textbf{NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)}\par

The NIST AI RMF \cite{nist_ai_rmf} organises AI risk governance around four functions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GOVERN}: organisational policies, accountability structures, and risk culture for AI use.
  \item \textbf{MAP}: contextualising the AI application, identifying potential harms, and categorising risk.
  \item \textbf{MEASURE}: quantifying and evaluating identified AI risks using defined metrics and testing.
  \item \textbf{MANAGE}: prioritising, responding to, and monitoring AI risks over time.
\end{itemize}

The framework's four pillars map directly: Pillar~I (Governance) operationalises GOVERN; Pillar~IV (Risk Management) covers MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE.

\noindent\textbf{ISO/IEC Standards}\par

\textbf{ISO/IEC 42001:2023} \cite{iso42001} (AI Management Systems) requires organisations to conduct an AI risk assessment before deployment (Clause~6), maintain documented human oversight mechanisms (Annex~A.6), and evaluate AI system impacts on data subjects (Annex~A.9). These requirements are satisfied by Pillar~II (human-in-the-loop), Pillar~III (accountability record), and Pillar~IV (risk register).

\textbf{ISO/IEC 27001:2022} \cite{iso27001} (Information Security Management Systems) requires information classification (Annex~A.8.2), data masking controls (A.8.11), and documented information security policies (A.5.1). The document risk classification scheme in Pillar~II directly implements A.8.2; the audit log satisfies the evidence requirements of A.8.15.

\subsection{Framework Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The framework is organized as a \textbf{governance spine} (Pillar~I) that gives formal authority to three operational pillars, all feeding into a hybrid adoption lifecycle. The central design principle is stated once here and governs every subsequent decision in the paper:

\begin{principlebox}[Architectural Principle]
\textbf{AI suggests, humans approve, the organization remains accountable.} The GenAI tool is a detection assistant, not a decision authority. Every publication decision is owned by a named human; every decision is recorded by the audit log; and the governance policy, not the tool's confidence score, defines when human override is mandatory.
\end{principlebox}

\noindent\textbf{The Governance Spine}\par

Pillar~I is the load-bearing structure. It defines the policy mandate, the accountability hierarchy, and the RACI matrix that authorizes the other three pillars to operate. Without it, the other pillars have no formal standing: the audit log is not required, the review gate can be bypassed, and the risk register has no accountable owner.

\noindent\textbf{The Three Operational Pillars}\par

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pillar~II: Human-in-the-Loop Controls} classifies every inbound document into one of three risk tiers (standard, high, very high) and routes it through the corresponding review gate. The lowest tier is named \emph{standard}, not \emph{low}, because every document entering the workflow already contains personal data subject to GDPR; there is no tier at which redaction review is optional. High-risk and very-high-risk documents cannot be published without explicit, documented human approval. GenAI output is a first-pass recommendation, not a final decision.

  \item \textbf{Pillar~III: Audit Logging} records every action taken on every document: the GenAI redaction proposals, each human review step, the final approval or rejection, and the identity and timestamp of every actor. The log is immutable, queryable, and retained as regulatory evidence.

  \item \textbf{Pillar~IV: Risk Management} maintains a living risk register for the GenAI tool itself, tracks residual risk after controls are applied, and triggers escalation when thresholds are crossed. It also specifies the manual fallback process for system unavailability.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:docflow} shows how a document moves from draft to publication. The flow is linear for standard-risk documents; high-risk and very-high-risk documents add the mandatory approval step (Step~5) before the audit token is issued.

\noindent\textbf{Hybrid Adoption Lifecycle}\par

The framework is designed predictively (all four pillars are defined in full before the pilot begins), but deployed iteratively: a controlled pilot tests the workflow on a defined document set, generates KPI data, and informs calibration of the risk-tier thresholds and review workloads before organization-wide rollout. This sequencing reduces adoption risk without sacrificing architectural coherence; it follows the hybrid lifecycle approach described in Section~\ref{sec:foundations} \cite{falchi_slides}.
